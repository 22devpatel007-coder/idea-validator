# Research Methodology

## Source hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order, depending on the claim:

1. Primary evidence: official product/pricing pages, company filings, government statistics, legal texts, product documentation, customer-provided data.
2. High-quality secondary evidence: reputable industry publications, established research organizations, credible journalism.
3. Community signals: forums, review sites, public discussions, app reviews. Treat as anecdotal and potentially biased.
4. Search snippets and unsourced claims: discovery leads only, not sufficient evidence.

## Source evaluation

For each important source, assess:
- Authority: who published it?
- Recency: when was it published or updated?
- Relevance: does it match the target geography, customer, and use case?
- Directness: does it directly support the claim?
- Incentive: does the publisher have a commercial or advocacy motive?
- Method: is the underlying method explained?

## Evidence labels

- Verified fact: directly supported by a credible source.
- Supported estimate: calculated from sourced inputs with assumptions shown.
- Inference: reasonable conclusion from evidence, but not directly established.
- Untested assumption: currently lacks sufficient supporting evidence.

## Search strategy

Search distinct question groups rather than repeating broad queries:
- problem and customer pain;
- customer workarounds;
- direct competitors;
- substitutes and adjacent categories;
- pricing and packaging;
- industry structure and regulation;
- distribution channels;
- technical dependencies;
- negative reviews and failure stories.

Use the target market's language and geography. For example, an idea aimed at Indian independent clinics should not rely entirely on US enterprise healthcare data.

## Avoid common research errors

- Do not use total addressable market as proof of reachable demand.
- Do not treat search volume as purchase intent.
- Do not treat competitor presence as proof the idea is impossible.
- Do not treat a lack of obvious competitors as proof of an empty market.
- Do not extrapolate a few reviews into universal customer opinion.
- Do not assume a feature gap is a business opportunity.
- Do not confuse user, buyer, approver, and payer.
- Do not use outdated pricing without labeling it.
- Do not infer a company's revenue from its customer count unless the calculation is clearly labeled and defensible.

## Research stopping rule

Stop when:
- the major perspectives have been addressed;
- additional searches are producing repetitive evidence;
- remaining uncertainties are better resolved by real-world tests;
- or tool/time limits prevent more research.

State what remains unknown. Do not hide gaps behind a polished report.
